What this lecture is

A way of thinking about a scraping task before you run it: three questions, asked in order, each with the law and the cases that answer it. You will leave able to classify a scenario and say which rule it runs into — which is what a supervisor, a client or a lawyer will ask you first.

What it is not. Legal advice. The cases are US, EU and Indian; the statutes are real; the dates and statuses were checked in August 2026. But every one of them turns on facts, and yours will differ. When a project touches personal data or a site that has told you to stop, ask someone whose job this is.
Why a data scientist needs this. Two of the four live bugs in the Lecture 2 scraper invented data silently. The legal failures in this lecture behave the same way: nothing crashes, the CSV looks fine, and the problem arrives months later as a letter.

robots.txt · 1 of 3

robots.txt: a 1994 request that became a 2022 standard One plain-text file at the root of a site, telling automatic clients which paths the owner would rather they left alone. Martijn Koster wrote the convention in 1994; the IETF published it as RFC 9309 in September 2022. It is the first thing to read, and the easiest to misread.

https://www.dailyledger.example/robots.txt

Allow: /api/public/
Crawl-delay: 10        # not in the RFC

User-agent: GPTBot
Disallow: /

User-agent: ClaudeBot
Disallow: /

Sitemap: https://www.dailyledger.example/sitemap.xml
Read it as the owner's statement of intent. Groups of rules per user-agent; the most specific group that matches your name applies, and * is the fallback. Everything not disallowed is allowed.

Two things the file tells a scraper that nothing else does. Which paths the owner considers off-limits — your /api/ replay from Lecture 1 is named here — and, where present, how fast they can bear to be crawled.

Look at the third line. The file says Allow: /api/public/, so /api/public/feed.xml should be fetchable. Python says False. Swap the two lines in the file and it says True — we tested both orders:
Disallow first, Allow second -> False Allow first, Disallow second -> True

robots.txt · 3 of 3

What robots.txt is not: a lock, a law, or a contract Students overrate it in one direction and underrate it in the other. It enforces nothing — and courts still read it. Both halves matter.

Not a lock

Nothing stops a client that ignores it. In In re OpenAI Copyright Infringement Litigation (the Ziff Davis claims, S.D.N.Y., 15 Dec 2025) Judge Stein dismissed the theory that ignoring a Disallow: GPTBot line "circumvents a technological measure" under the DMCA: a robots.txt file no more controls access "than a sign requesting that visitors 'keep off the grass' effectively controls access to a lawn." The copyright claims themselves continued.

Not a law

No statute in the US, EU or India says robots.txt must be obeyed. RFC 9309 is an IETF standard for how to parse it, not an obligation to honour it.

Not, on its own, a contract

You never agreed to it. Whether it can be the basis of a unilateral contract or of "notice" for a trespass claim is argued in the literature; it has not been the deciding fact in a reported scraping case.

And yet: it is evidence, in both directions

When a court asks whether access was authorised (Q1) or whether a scraper acted in good faith, robots.txt is one of the first documents produced. Honouring it is the cheapest evidence of good faith you will ever generate. Ignoring it — especially a line that names your bot — is the cheapest evidence against you, and it is what turns "I didn't know" into "you were told".

Where it sits on the map. Q1, next to Terms of Service: both are the owner stating what is permitted, neither is a gate. The gate — the thing that turns scraping into unauthorised access — is a login, a block, or a letter. Slide 12.
The rule for this course. Read it before the first request, honour Disallow for your UA and for *, treat Crawl-delay as a minimum, and log which version you read and when. If the path you need is disallowed, that is not a technical obstacle to route around — it is your answer, and the next step is an email to the owner.
llms.txt

llms.txt is a menu, not a gate Students conflate it with robots.txt constantly, and the names invite it. They are not siblings. One tells crawlers what to leave alone; the other tells language models where the good bits are.

What it is

A proposal by Jeremy Howard (Answer.AI), 3 Sept 2024: serve a Markdown file at /llms.txt — a title, a summary, and a list of links to clean, LLM-friendly versions of your key pages — so an assistant with a small context window can find documentation without parsing navigation, ads and scripts.

# Daily Ledger
> Financial news and market data, Chennai.

## Docs
- [API reference](https://www.dailyledger.example/docs/api.md)
- [Data dictionary](https://www.dailyledger.example/docs/fields.md)
What it is not. It contains no directives. It cannot allow, deny, or rate-limit anything. No crawler treats it as a permission file, and as of Aug 2026 no major model provider has committed to honouring it at all. Writing Disallow into it does nothing.

Terms of Service · 1 of 2

Terms of Service: the difference between a sign and a signature Nearly every site has a page forbidding "automated access". Whether it binds you depends on one thing: did you ever agree to it — and courts have been specific about what counts.

Browsewrap — the sign

A "Terms" link in the footer. You never clicked anything. Specht v. Netscape (2d Cir. 2002) and Nguyen v. Barnes & Noble (9th Cir. 2014): a link the user had no reason to see does not create a contract — there must be actual or constructive notice. A scraper that only ever fetched public pages has typically given no assent at all.

Clickwrap — the signature

A checkbox or "I agree" button at sign-up. Reliably enforceable. The moment you create an account to scrape — or log in with one — you are inside the contract, and its anti-scraping clause is a promise you made. hiQ ended here; Reddit v. Anthropic is being fought here.

What a ToS breach actually is

A contract claim — damages, injunction, account termination. Not a crime. After Van Buren (slide 10), breaking a purpose rule you agreed to is not, by itself, "unauthorised access" under the CFAA. That is the single most important thing the last decade of cases settled.

But do not read that as "ToS is toothless". It is the claim that survived in hiQ — a $500,000 judgment and a permanent injunction — and in the EU, Ryanair v. PR Aviation (slide 11) made a website's terms the winning theory. A contract you actually agreed to is the most ordinary law there is.
Practical test, in order. (1) Did I log in or create an account? If yes, the ToS binds me; read its scraping clause. (2) If no, am I being shown the terms in a way a court would call notice? (3) Either way, has the site told me to stop? That last one moves us from Q1-contract to Q1-access.
Terms of Service · 2 of 2

What a ToS can still do after hiQ — and whom it cannot reach Two rulings, thirteen months apart, in the same district court, draw the line cleanly: the terms bind the member, and stop at the stranger.

hiQ v. LinkedIn · N.D. Cal. · Nov 2022

On summary judgment, the court held LinkedIn's User Agreement provisions against scraping and fake profiles enforceable in contract. hiQ had used fake accounts (and so had logged in). A month later the parties filed a consent judgment: $500,000 against hiQ, a permanent injunction, and deletion of all scraped data and code.

bindsanyone who accepted the terms — which includes anyone who logged in to scrape.

Put together. Whether the anti-scraping clause applies to you is decided by a fact you control: did you authenticate? Scraping a public page while logged out is a different legal position from scraping the same page while logged in — even if the bytes are identical. Do not log in to scrape unless the terms you accepted permit it; and if a project needs an account, that is the moment to read the clause, not after.

Meta v. Bright Data · N.D. Cal. · 23 Jan 2024

Judge Chen granted summary judgment to Bright Data on Meta's contract claim: the evidence showed only logged-off scraping of public pages, and a user who is not logged in is not "using" the service in the sense the terms govern. Meta dropped the case and waived appeal in Feb 2024.

does not reacha stranger fetching public pages without an account — the ordinary Lecture 2 scraper.

Caveat for the careful reader. Both are district-court rulings in one US circuit. Other judges can and do differ; the EU's Ryanair line is less forgiving. The direction of travel is consistent, the law is not uniform.
The decided cases · 1 of 3 · Q1, access

Four cases, one argument: the gate is up, or the gate is down The facts of each case are in the gallery three slides on. What a gallery cannot show you is that these four are a sequence — each one answers the question the previous one left open.

1Van Buren (2021) draws the line

A police officer looked up a licence plate he was entitled to look up, for money he was not entitled to take. The Supreme Court held the CFAA punishes going where you may not go, not going where you may go for the wrong reason. Gates, not purposes.

leaves openWhat counts as a gate on a page nobody has to log in to?

2hiQ (2019–2022) answers it — and loses anyway

The Ninth Circuit: a public page is not a gate, so scraping it is not "without authorization", C&D letter or no. hiQ won the point. Then the district court found it had scraped with fake accounts — so it had logged in, so the User Agreement bound it. $500,000 and a permanent injunction.

leaves openWhat if the site blocks you and you go around the block?

33Taps (2013) answers that

Craigslist sent a C&D and blocked 3Taps' IP addresses. 3Taps kept scraping through proxies. Judge Breyer: a C&D plus a block is a clear enough revocation that continued access by any route is unauthorised. Settled for $1,000,000.

leaves openDoes a contract reach someone who never had an account at all?

4Meta v. Bright Data (2024) closes the loop

Meta lost on contract: the evidence showed only logged-off scraping of public pages, and someone who is not logged in is not "using" the service the terms govern. Meta dropped the case and waived appeal.

Read the four together and the rule is one sentence. Public page, logged out, nothing circumvented, nobody has told you to stop — you are outside all four cases. Break any one of those and you are inside one of them, and the deck can tell you which.
The decided cases · 2 of 3 · Q2, what is protectable

Three cases, one argument: facts are free, so the terms get harsh This chain runs the opposite way round from the last one. It starts with good news for a scraper and ends by explaining why every data site you will ever touch has a hostile terms page.

1Feist (1991) · facts cannot be owned

Feist copied 1,309 listings out of a phone directory. The US Supreme Court, unanimously: facts are never copyrightable, because nobody authors a fact. A compilation is protected only in its selection or arrangement, and only where that shows a modicum of creativity. Alphabetical order shows none. Sweat of the brow is not a theory of copyright.

2EBC v. Modak (2008) · India says the same

The Supreme Court of India rejected sweat of the brow and adopted a skill-and-judgment standard, following Feist and CCH Canadian. Raw judgments: no copyright. Routine copy-editing: not original. Only a narrow set of genuinely judgment-laden additions survived.

andIndia has no sui generis database right — the EU's separate protection for investment has no Indian counterpart.

3Ryanair (2015) · so the contract does the work

PR Aviation scraped Ryanair's fares. The CJEU held the Database Directive's user protections apply only to databases that qualify for copyright or the sui generis right — and Ryanair's fares qualified for neither. A database outside the Directive is outside its user protections too. Nothing stopped Ryanair's contract from binding a user who accepted it.

The counter-intuitive rule, and the point of the chain. The weaker the intellectual property in the data, the freer the owner is to rely on terms. Prices, fares, index levels, listings: no copyright, no database right, so the terms of use are not one weapon among several — they are the only one. That is why they read the way they do, and why the contract question on slide 15 matters more than the copyright one.

Factor 4 is the whole difference. Does the output compete with the input? A model that writes new text is not the books it read. A research tool trained on headnotes to answer the same queries is the headnotes' market. Same four factors, opposite facts on one of them.

The second lesson in Bartz is the one for scrapers

Alsup held the training fair — and held that building a permanent library from roughly seven million pirated copies was not excused by it. That piece settled for $1.5 billion, about $3,000 per work; final approval July 2026.

The transformative use did not launder the acquisition. Translated: a legitimate analysis does not retroactively make an unauthorised scrape authorised. How you got the copy is judged on its own.

Two still pending, in different boxes

Anthropic — deliberately no copyright claim: contract, trespass and unfair competition, remanded to state court in March 2026. Anything you read calling either "decided" is wrong.

India · 1 of 5 · the access statute

IT Act 2000: §43 is civil and broad, §66 is criminal and narrow Everything before this slide is American or European law, because that is where the scraping cases have been fought. You will work under Indian law. Here is what it actually says — and, more importantly, what it has never been asked.

§43 — civil liability, no intent required

"If any person, without permission of the owner or any other person who is in charge of a computer, computer system or computer network — (a) accesses or secures access to it … (b) downloads, copies or extracts any data, computer data base or information … he shall be liable to pay compensation to the person so affected."

Read the verbs. Not hacks, not breaks into: accesses, downloads, copies, extracts. Every one of those describes a scraper doing its ordinary job. The whole section turns on four words — without permission of the owner — and nothing in the Act defines them. The ₹1 crore ceiling was removed by the 2008 amendment; compensation is now uncapped.

§66 — the criminal version

Doing any §43 act "dishonestly or fraudulently": up to three years, or a fine up to ₹5 lakh, or both. Those two words are not decoration — they carry their Penal Code meanings, now Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita 2023 §2(7) and §2(9): dishonestly = intending wrongful gain to one person or wrongful loss to another; fraudulently = intending to defraud. Absent that intent, §66 does not arise at all, however much data you copied.

The gap that matters

There is no reported Indian judgment applying §43 to scraping a public website. "Without permission" has never been given a Van Buren-style reading here — nobody has told us whether permission means the access controls the owner configured or the words the owner published.

The Indian prosecutions that do exist — ticketing bots, account takeovers — all involve real circumvention: stolen credentials, defeated CAPTCHAs, forged sessions. They answer an easier question than ours.

What "untested" costs you. It cuts both ways. There is no Indian authority holding that public scraping violates §43 — and none holding that it does not. A project cannot budget on either reading. Build so that you win under the stricter one.
India · 2 of 5 · the contract question

"I never clicked I agree" — does an Indian court care? Slide 8 gave you the American answer: browsewrap is a sign, clickwrap is a signature. India has no scraping case on the point, but it has 150 years of contract law, and that law is not ambiguous about what makes an agreement.

Indian Contract Act 1872 — what a contract needs

§2(a)–(e): a proposal, accepted, becomes a promise; promises supported by consideration are an agreement. §13: parties must agree "upon the same thing in the same sense" — consensus ad idem. §10: agreements are contracts only if made by free consent.

A terms page you never opened, linked from a footer your scraper never rendered, fails at the first step. There is no acceptance, so there is no promise, so there is nothing to breach. This is not a technicality; it is the definition.

IT Act §10A is sometimes quoted against this. It says a contract is not unenforceable merely because it was formed electronically. It validates the form. It does not manufacture the assent.

Where Indian law is settled. Clickwrap works. Trimex International v. Vedanta (2010, SC) confirms that no signature or paper is needed — an exchange showing agreement is enough. The corollary is the useful half: something must show agreement. A GET request shows nothing.

The three arguments on the other side

1 · Constructive notice. If you knew the terms existed — and a professional does — continued deliberate use can be pleaded as acceptance by conduct. US courts have bought this for a repeat commercial scraper (Register.com v. Verio, 2d Cir. 2004). Persuasive only in India, but it is the shape of the argument you will meet.

2 · You logged in once. Any account, ever, for any reason. Then you clicked, and the clause is yours.

3 · Contract is not the only theory. A site that loses on contract can still plead unjust enrichment or unfair competition — no agreement needed.

The practical test. Did I authenticate? If yes, the terms bind me and I read the scraping clause before I write the scraper. If no, the terms are the owner's stated wish — evidence of what they want, not a promise I made.
India · 3 of 5 · what you may keep

Copyright Act 1957: a database is protected, a price series is not India protects databases — as literary works, under the ordinary copyright section. What it refuses to protect is the effort of assembling facts. One Supreme Court judgment settles the whole question, and it is not a copyright case about the web at all.

The provisions

§2(o) defines "literary work" to include computer databases. §13 gives copyright to original literary works. So a database is copyrightable in principle — the entire fight is over the word original.

§52 is the exception list: fair dealing for private or personal use including research, criticism, review, reporting, and use in the course of instruction. Unlike US fair use it is a closed list, not a balancing test. A course project sits inside it comfortably; a commercial dataset does not.

Eastern Book Company v. D.B. Modak · (2008) 1 SCC 1

EBC published law reports and claimed copyright in judgments it had copy-edited. The Supreme Court rejected the "sweat of the brow" doctrine — labour and expense alone earn nothing — and adopted a modicum of creativity / skill and judgment standard, following Feist (US) and CCH Canadian. Raw judgments: no copyright. Only the genuinely creative editorial inputs were protected.

India has no sui generis database right

The EU's Database Directive gives a separate 15-year right to whoever made a substantial investment in obtaining or verifying the contents, whether or not anything creative happened. India never enacted an equivalent, and neither did the US.

So the European claim — you took a substantial part of the database I paid to build — has no Indian counterpart. Here the owner must show creativity, and a table sorted by date has none available to it.

Apply it to a price series. Dates, opens, closes, highs, lows: facts. Their arrangement is chronological, which is the only arrangement the data permits — no selection, no judgment, no creativity. Under EBC v. Modak there is nothing to infringe. Copy the text of a research note off the same site and the answer reverses completely.
India · 4 of 5 · the privacy statute

DPDP: "publicly available" is narrower than "on the internet" The Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 is India's first general data-protection statute. It has one exemption students reach for and misread. Start with the vocabulary, then read the exemption exactly.

TermMeaning
Personal dataAny data about an individual who is identifiable by or in relation to it. A name + phone on a listing is personal data. A company name is not.
Data PrincipalThe person the data is about.
Data FiduciaryWhoever decides the purpose and means of processing. If you scrape it and decide what to do with it, that is you.
ProcessingCollection, storage, analysis, sharing, deletion — all of it. Scraping is collection.
Lawful basis§6 consent, or a §7 "legitimate use" — a short closed list (the principal volunteered it to you for that purpose; state functions; employment; medical emergency, etc.). "Research" is not a free-standing legitimate use for a private party.
Data Protection BoardThe adjudicator. Constituted Nov 2025.

The exemption, read exactly · §3(c)(ii)

The Act does not apply to personal data that is "made or caused to be made publicly available by — (A) the Data Principal to whom such personal data relates; or (B) any other person who is under an obligation under any law… to make such personal data publicly available."

What that covers: a public post the person wrote; a public profile they filled in; a statutory register. What it does not cover: data about a person that someone else put online; data that is merely crawlable, indexed, or sitting in a third-party dataset. "I found it on the web" is not the test. "They published it about themselves" is.

Two more things to know. The Act applies to processing outside India if it concerns offering goods or services to people in India (§3(b)). And there is no general research exemption for individuals — the §17 research carve-out is for processing "necessary for research, archiving or statistical purposes" done under standards the Rules prescribe, not a blanket pass for a project notebook.

The habits that make the answer easy

Minimise: do not collect personal fields you do not need. Anonymise early: hash or drop names and phones before the data leaves your notebook. Purpose-bind: write the purpose down; delete when it is served. Document: source, date, what you removed. These four are the entire compliance story for most student projects, and they cost ten lines of pandas.

Verified Aug 2026, will drift. Phase dates come from the Rules as notified; if you are reading this after May 2027, everything is in force.
The question · 1 of 3 · the facts

NSE, NIFTY history, and a terms page you never read Everything in this lecture, applied to a single scenario. Read the facts slowly — each one is load-bearing, and changing any of them changes the answer.

The scenario

1  You run Python and BeautifulSoup against publicly accessible NSE pages, or against the internal API those pages call.

2  No login. No account, ever.

3  No CAPTCHA bypass, no circumvention of any kind. Nothing was defeated; the server simply answered.

4  You collect only NIFTY price and history data — dates and numbers.

5  NSE's Terms of Use explicitly prohibit scraping.

6  You never clicked "I agree."

Why this one. It is the most common shape of an Indian data-science scrape: a public market page, a factual series, and a hostile terms page nobody signed.
Three facts are doing almost all the work. Fact 2 (no login) decides the contract question on its own. Fact 3 (no circumvention) is what separates this from every Indian prosecution that exists. Fact 4 (numbers only) removes copyright and privacy from the case entirely. Change any one of them and you are answering a different question — which is why the answer on slide 21 comes with a list of what would flip it.

It is not one question. It is four.

"Is this legal?" cannot be answered because four separate bodies of law are being asked at once, and they do not agree with each other. Separate them and each has a clean answer — three of them confidently, one of them not.

Head of claimLawThe question it actually asks
AccessIT Act 2000 §43 (civil), §66 (criminal) Was the fetch "without permission of the owner"? Facts 1, 2, 3, 5
ContractIndian Contract Act 1872 §2, §10, §13 Did the Terms of Use ever become a promise you made? Facts 5, 6
CopyingCopyright Act 1957 §2(o), §13, §52 Is a NIFTY price series someone's original work? Fact 4
Keep & useDPDP Act 2023 Is any of it about a living person? No. Prices are not personal data — this head drops out entirely. Fact 4
Note what fact 1 hides. "Public page" and "internal API" are technically identical — both unauthenticated HTTP — but they are not the same fact in a courtroom. We come back to this on slide 21.
The question · 2 of 3 · argued both ways

The case for lawful, and the case against — argued properly Both columns are real arguments a competent lawyer would run. If you can only make one of them, you do not yet understand the question.

It is lawful

1 · The server granted permission. §43 asks whether access was "without permission of the owner". The owner's computer answered an unauthenticated request with 200 OK. In a statute about computer access, permission is most naturally read off the access controls the owner configured, not off prose published elsewhere on the site. This is hiQ's reasoning, and it is the only reading that makes the rest of §43 coherent.

2 · Nothing was circumvented. No credential, no CAPTCHA, no token, no block routed around. §43's neighbouring limbs describe breaking in. Reading a page the server hands to anyone is the opposite of that.

3 · There is no contract. Acceptance is an element, not a formality (Contract Act §2, §13). No click, no assent, no consensus ad idem, no promise to breach. No Indian court has enforced pure browsewrap against a non-user.

4 · There is no copyright. Dates and closing levels are facts; their order is chronological and therefore uncreative. EBC v. Modak killed sweat-of-the-brow, and India has no database right to fall back on.

5 · §66 cannot arise. No wrongful gain, no wrongful loss, no intent to defraud. The criminal door is shut on the facts.

Notice what this column never claims. Not that the terms are unfair, not that scraping is useful, not that NSE was unharmed. Only three factual assertions — no gate, no click, no creativity — each provable from your own logs.

It is not

1 · "Permission" may mean what the owner said. The section reads without permission of the owner — not without circumventing an access control. If a court takes permission to be the owner's stated position, the Terms of Use are that statement, and §43(b) — downloads, copies or extracts — is made out without any break-in at all. §43 is civil and needs no dishonesty. This is the strongest argument against, and nothing in Indian law currently defeats it.

2 · The internal API is a worse fact than the page. A linked page is held out to the public. An undocumented endpoint is not held out to anyone. Technically identical; rhetorically not. With no Van Buren to constrain it, a court may find "you used an interface never offered to you" persuasive.

3 · Notice can stand in for a click. A professional who knows the terms exist and scrapes anyway can be met with acceptance by conduct (Register.com v. Verio). Persuasive authority only — but it is the argument.

4 · Contract is not the only theory. Losing on contract still leaves unjust enrichment and unfair competition, neither of which needs your agreement — and NSE licenses market data commercially, so there is a priced product to point at.

Notice what this column never alleges either. Not hacking. Every argument here is built from facts you have already admitted — which is why "I didn't circumvent anything" is a weaker defence than it feels. Nobody said you did.
The question · 3 of 3 · the conclusion

Lawful — but on an argument, not on a rule Head by head, with the confidence attached to each. Notice that the one unsettled answer is also the only one that could cost you money.

HeadVerdictReasoning
§66 criminalNo liability. Confident. Requires dishonest or fraudulent intent — wrongful gain or wrongful loss. Collecting published index levels for analysis is neither.
§43 civilProbably none. Untested. The better reading of "without permission" is the technical one, and hiQ supports it. But no Indian court has said so, and the rival reading is available on the words. This is where the whole risk sits.
ContractNot bound. Confident — conditionally. No acceptance, so no contract. Conditional because it reverses completely the moment an account exists.
CopyrightNo infringement. Confident. Facts, chronologically arranged. EBC v. Modak; no sui generis database right in India.
DPDPNot engaged. No personal data in a price series.
Say it in one sentence. Logged out, nothing circumvented, facts only, no personal data, not redistributed — that sits inside every settled rule in Indian law, and outside only a reading of §43 that no court has yet adopted.

Three things that flip the answer

1 · You log in. Any account, any reason, once. Now you clicked, the Terms bind you, and the anti-scraping clause is a promise you made. This is how hiQ ended — $500,000 and a permanent injunction.

2 · They block you, or write to you, and you continue. The 3Taps pattern. "Without permission" stops being an argument and becomes trivially provable, and your proxy rotation is the evidence.

3 · You redistribute or sell it. Market data is a licensed product. Unjust enrichment and unfair competition do not need copyright, and do not need your agreement either.

The honest split in the answer. The public page is the strong case. The undocumented endpoint is the same technical position with worse optics and no Indian authority to lean on. If you only need one of the two, take the page.
Not legal advice. Every case here turns on its facts, and yours will differ. Where a project touches money, personal data, or a site that has told you to stop, the correct next step is a person whose job this is.
GDPR · the EU regime

GDPR: "it was on a public page" is not a lawful basis If any row is about a person in the EU — or your client is European — the GDPR applies to you in Chennai. It is also the template most companies' internal data policies copy, so its vocabulary is worth owning whether or not it binds you.

The four articles a scraper actually runs into

Art. 3 · reach. Applies to processing about people in the EU by anyone, anywhere, if you offer them goods or services or monitor their behaviour. Your location is irrelevant; the data subject's is decisive.

Art. 6 · lawful basis. You need one before you collect: consent, contract, legal obligation, vital interests, public task, or legitimate interests balanced against the person's rights. There is no public-data exemption in the GDPR at all — unlike DPDP §3(c)(ii), which at least has a narrow one.

Art. 14 · notice for data not obtained from the person. Which is every scrape. You must tell each person who you are, why you have their data, and what rights they have, within a month — unless that is impossible or disproportionate. Regulators have fined scrapers on exactly this article; the Clearview AI decisions across several member states are the reference set.

Art. 5 · principles. Minimisation, purpose limitation, storage limitation, accuracy. The habits from the checklist, written into statute.

Special categories · Art. 9

Health, biometrics, sexual orientation, religion, politics, trade union membership, race. Processing is prohibited by default and needs one of ten narrow gateways — not merely a lawful basis. A scrape that incidentally captures any of these has a much harder problem than one that captures names.

Art. 9(2)(e) lifts the ban where the person has manifestly made the data public themselves. That is the closest thing to a public-data exemption in the whole Regulation, and it is far narrower than it sounds: it needs a deliberate act of publication by the person, not by a site about them.

Why a BTech project should care. Not because Brussels will fine you. Because "lawful basis", "minimisation", "purpose limitation" and "data subject rights" are the four phrases every data-governance policy you will ever be handed is written in. Learn them once and you can read all of them.
HIPAA · health data is its own regime

HIPAA: not a privacy law, a sectoral one — and that is the trap Students reach for HIPAA whenever health appears. It is much narrower than that, and much stricter inside its boundary. Knowing which side of the boundary you are on is the whole skill.

What it actually governs

Not health data in general. It governs protected health information held by covered entities — providers, health plans, clearinghouses — and by their business associates under contract.

A student scraping a public site is normally neither. Scrape clinic listings and HIPAA does not reach you. Get hired to process a hospital's records and it reaches you as a business associate, immediately and fully.

De-identification · §164.514. Two routes only: Safe Harbor, which requires stripping 18 enumerated identifiers — names, all geography below state, all dates finer than a year, phone, email, IP, device and record numbers, faces, ages over 89 — or Expert Determination, a qualified statistician certifying the re-identification risk is very small. "I dropped the name column" is neither route.

Where a scraper meets it anyway

Reviews of clinics that name a condition. Patient forums. Fundraising pages for treatment. Doctor-rating sites where the reviewer is the patient. None of it is HIPAA-regulated at source — and all of it is health data about identifiable people, which means DPDP and the GDPR are engaged even where HIPAA is not.

How India compares

The DPDP Act has no separate health category — all personal data is treated alike, with children's data as the one special case. The EU is the opposite: health is an Art. 9 special category with a default prohibition. So for the same hospital dataset, the strictest regime is European, not American.

One line across all three regimes. Privacy law does not ask how politely you got the data. It asks what you are doing with it, and why you are allowed to.
Privacy · worked example

Forty business cards with phone numbers: which regime, what must you do This is the Lecture 2 scraper — a listings page, ten to forty cards, each with a name, a locality, a phone number, a rating, a score. Run it through the three questions as a supervisor would.

QuestionAnswer for this scrape
Q1 accessPublic page, no login, one request per page, saved snapshot, robots.txt read first. Gate up. If the site later 403s you — stop (slide 10).
Q2 copyNames, localities, numbers, ratings: facts. The long descriptions: expression — extract a feature, drop the text. Do not republish the page.
Q3 keepMost cards are businesses — company name, office number: not personal data. But a sole trader's card — "Ravi K, 98xxx xxxxx, Velachery" — is a living person's name and phone. That column is personal data under DPDP and GDPR.
§3(c)(ii)?The trader listed themselves on the directory to be contacted — arguably data "made publicly available by the Data Principal". Plausible, untested, and it does not help the rows a third party posted. Do not build a project on an exemption no court has read yet.

What you actually do

1. Ask whether the project needs the phone number. A ratings-vs-response-time analysis does not. Drop the column at parse time.

2. If a contact field is genuinely needed (say, a de-duplication key), hash it with a salt you keep out of the repo, and keep the raw value nowhere.

3. Separate the business from the person: business name is a feature; proprietor name is not — drop or pseudonymise.

4. Write one paragraph in the README: source, date, robots.txt status, fields kept, fields removed and why, purpose, deletion date.

5. Never publish the raw snapshot. The processed, minimised CSV is the artefact.

df = df.drop(columns=['phone'])            # 1 · minimise
df['owner_id'] = df['owner'].map(
    lambda s: hashlib.sha256((SALT+s).encode()).hexdigest()[:12])
df = df.drop(columns=['owner'])            # 2–3 · pseudonymise
df['desc_len'] = df['description'].str.len()
df = df.drop(columns=['description'])      # Q2 · feature, not text
Ten lines of pandas turn an awkward dataset into a defensible one. The time to write them is inside the scraper, not after the CSV has been emailed around.

Discipline · 2 of 3 · one page to lift

A checklist you can paste into the project README Before, during, after. If a box cannot be ticked, the project has a design problem, not a paperwork problem.

Before the first request

☐ Is there an API, feed, bulk download or llms.txt? Use it.
☐ Read robots.txt; record the date and the lines that apply to me.
☐ Read the ToS scraping clause. Do I need an account? If yes, does the clause permit this?
☐ List the fields I need. Mark which are personal data. Decide now what I will drop or hash.
☐ Write the purpose in one sentence, and a deletion date.
☐ UA string with project name and contact.

While it runs

☐ One request per page, timeout on every request, backoff on 429/5xx, never on 4xx.
☐ Logged out. No proxies. No stealth flags beyond what rendering needs.
☐ Dated snapshot to disk; parse from disk.
☐ On 403 / captcha / ban: stop. Do not retry from elsewhere.
☐ Provenance row per fetch.
☐ Minimise at parse time — the raw personal field never reaches the DataFrame.

Before anyone else sees it

☐ Lecture 1's six assertions pass.
☐ No column holds text copied whole from the source unless I can say why.
☐ No column holds a living person's name, phone, email or address in the clear.
☐ README: source, date, robots.txt status, fields kept/removed, purpose, deletion date, the licence I am claiming for the CSV.
☐ Raw snapshot stays private.
☐ I can say the one-sentence justification from slide 21 out loud.

For the course project this checklist, filled in, is submitted with the data. A dataset without it is an unverified scrape, the same way a DataFrame without the six assertions is an unvalidated one.
Practice

Five exercises Numbers 2 and 5 are the ones that will change how you scrape. Do them before the project proposal.

01Read three real robots.txt files

In your own browser, outside class, open /robots.txt on a news site, an e-commerce site and a government portal. For each: which paths are disallowed for *; is there a Crawl-delay; are GPTBot/ClaudeBot/CCBot named; is there a Sitemap. One table, nine rows.

02Break the standard library, then fix it

Reproduce slide 5: a robots.txt with one Allow inside one Disallow, both orders, through urllib.robotparser. Then install protego and run the same four checks. Write the unit test that would have caught the difference, and say in a sentence which direction the stdlib errs in and why that is tolerable.

03Classify five scenarios

For each, say which box (Q1/Q2/Q3), which case or statute governs, and whether it is settled: (a) a logged-out scrape of public product prices; (b) the same after a 403; (c) copying full reviews into a Kaggle dataset; (d) a scraper that logs in with a shared class account; (e) a CSV of sole-trader names and phones from a directory. One paragraph each.

04Map a real ToS

Pick one site you intend to use for the project. Find its terms. Quote the clause on automated access. Decide: browsewrap or clickwrap for a logged-out visitor? Does the clause bind you, and under which case? Would it change if you created an account? Cite the slide.

05The ethics paragraph, and the ten lines

For your project dataset, fill in the slide-24 checklist, then write the README paragraph: source, date, robots.txt status, fields kept and removed and why, purpose, deletion date, licence. Then add the minimise/pseudonymise/feature-extract lines from slide 24 inside the scraper, and submit the before/after column list. The paragraph and the diff are the deliverable, not the CSV.

Ground rules, for the last time. No live scraping during these exercises except the three robots.txt files in (1), read in a browser. Everything else runs on inline strings and the saved snapshots you already have. And if a site has said no — in a file, a header, a letter or a 403 — that is the end of the exercise, not the start of a workaround.
