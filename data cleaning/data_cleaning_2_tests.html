The limit, stated first

Tests can rule out MCAR. Nothing can confirm MNAR. Worth knowing before you spend an hour on the arithmetic.

What the tests reach

What you can do

Test whether missingness is related to the columns you observed. If it is, MCAR is out. That test is real, standard, and worth running.

What you cannot do

Test whether missingness is related to the values you did not observe. The comparison needs those values. MNAR is argued for from how the data was collected.

Rubin's framework is a set of assumptions, and only one of the three leaves a signature the data can carry.

Three lines, three mechanisms. The middle one reads age, which the file contains. The last reads health, which the file is about to lose.
A test that marks gender MCAR and the other two not-MCAR has done its job. The open question is whether anything separates the last two lines.
Little's MCAR test · 1 of 2 · Little (1988), JASA

Compare the group means, pattern by pattern Under MCAR the rows in every missingness pattern are a random slice of the same population, so their means on the observed columns should agree.

The idea in one line

Split the rows by which columns are missing. If the groups differ on the columns they all share, missingness is carrying information.

Every row belongs to one pattern. With k columns there are at most 2k of them, and the survey uses all 8 of its possible 3-column patterns.
The test pools all patterns into one number, so it answers "is anything non-random anywhere", and does not say which column is the problem.

The statistic

d² = ∑j nj (x̄j − μ̂j)′ Σ̂j−1 (x̄j − μ̂j) j each missingness pattern nj rows in that pattern x̄j its mean on the columns it observes μ̂, Σ̂ ML estimates from all the data
  • A Mahalanobis distance per pattern, weighted by how many rows it holds.
  • Under MCAR it follows χ² with ∑j kj − k degrees of freedom, where kj counts the columns pattern j observes.
  • Large d² means the pattern means disagree by more than sampling noise.
Little's MCAR test · 2 of 2 · run on survey.csv

chi2 = 73.96 on 8 df, p = 8e-13 Run twice: once on all four numeric columns, once on the column we know is MCAR.

Where each statistic falls under the null
All four columns together: MCAR is rejected. Correct, and expected, since two of the three mechanisms we planted are not MCAR.
gender on its own: chi2 = 2.75, df = 2, p = 0.25. The test cannot reject MCAR for the column that is genuinely MCAR.

Reading a p-value you failed to reject

p = 0.25 says the data holds no evidence against MCAR. With 28 missing values it also holds little power to find any. Absence of evidence, at n = 28.

Little's MCAR test · what it assumes

Four conditions, and what breaks when they fail The test is standard and it is fragile. Check these before quoting the p-value.

AssumptionWhat happens when it fails
Multivariate normality The χ² reference is wrong. Income here has skew 1.91. Take logs (skew 0.05) and the statistic moves to 80.95, p = 3e-14, so the verdict survives. Worth checking on any skewed column before quoting a p-value.
Enough rows per pattern Σ̂j is estimated per pattern. The survey has one row in the all-three-missing pattern, and a pattern that thin contributes noise.
Sample size Large n rejects on differences too small to matter; small n misses real ones. Under 200 rows the test has little power. It answers "is there a difference", never "is it big enough to care about".
MCAR is the only alternative Rejection means not-MCAR. It says nothing about whether MAR or MNAR produced the pattern, which is the question you actually have.
In Python. There is no Little's test in scipy or scikit-learn. R has naniar::mcar_test and MissMech; the version here is forty lines of numpy, given in the notebook for this lecture.

When the permutation test earns its cost

Skewed or heavy-tailed columns, small samples, and statistics with no tractable null such as a median gap or a Gini difference. It also handles any statistic you can write as a function of the labels.

The result that matters is on the last two rows. The MAR column and the MNAR column both reject, at similar strength. Nothing in either number says which is which.
Logistic regression on the missingness indicator · 1 of 2

Model the missingness itself Turn "is this cell blank" into a 0/1 outcome and predict it from the columns you have.

R = s.income.isna().astype(int)

# logit P(R=1) = b0 + b1*age + b2*education
fit(X=s[["age","education_years"]], y=R)

Why this beats a t-test

It takes every observed column at once, so it separates a real driver from a column that merely correlates with one. Age and education move together here; the model says which one carries the missingness.

Read the coefficient on each predictor, and the likelihood-ratio test of the whole model against an intercept only.
ResultWhat it supports
No predictor significant Consistent with MCAR. Nothing you measured predicts who went missing.
Some predictor significant Consistent with MAR. The missingness is systematic and the driver is in your file, so imputation and weighting can use it.
The MNAR case Produces either of the two rows above. The driver is a column you do not have, and its correlation with a column you do have decides which.
Fit one model per column with gaps. Predictors must be columns that are complete, or you are modelling one gap with another.

Health missingness is driven by the health score. Age looks guilty because older respondents score lower, so age inherits the signal. Every test sees the inherited signal and reports MAR.
Act on that verdict and you impute health from age, which fills the withheld low scores with the average of the people who answered. That is the bias from Lecture 1, now with a p-value behind it.
Pattern-mixture models · 1 of 2

Pattern mixture is a framework, not a test It gives you a place to write the assumption down and a way to price it.

The split

Model the responders and the non-responders as two populations, then recombine them weighted by how many of each you have.

μ = (1 − π) μobs + π μmis π fraction missing 0.360 μobs mean of responders 87.0 μmis mean of the rest unobservable
Two of the three are in the data. The third is the whole problem, and no amount of arithmetic produces it.

The sensitivity parameter

Write μmis = μobs + δ. Then μ = μobs + πδ, and the entire MNAR problem is one number you have to supply.

δ you assumeWhat you are claiming
0Non-responders look like responders. This is the MAR answer, and it is what every imputation method defaults to.
−13Those who withheld score 13 points lower. A claim about behaviour, defended from how the survey was run.
Sources for δ: a follow-up study, an administrative record, a clinician's judgement, or a range you report rather than a value you pick.

What you report

Not a point. A range of δ you can defend, the estimate at each end, and whether your conclusion changes anywhere inside it. If it does not, the MNAR worry is priced and closed.

Choosing among them

4 tests and what each is for Run them in this order. Stop when the answer stops changing what you would do.

TestQuestion it answers AssumesUse it when
t-test / ANOVA
per column pair		 Do the missing and observed rows differ on one other column? normality, equal-ish variance First look. Cheap, and it names the column that differs.
Permutation
per column pair		 Same question, without a distributional claim. exchangeable labels Skewed or small data, or a statistic with no closed-form null.
Little's MCAR
whole dataset		 Is anything non-random anywhere across all patterns? multivariate normality, n > 200 One summary number for a report. It will not name the culprit.
Logistic on R
per column		 Which observed columns predict the missingness, holding the others fixed? the usual logistic conditions The one that pays. Its predictors are what your imputation model needs.
Pattern mixture
per column		 How far could the answer move if the missing values differ? a δ you supply Whenever MNAR is plausible. It prices the doubt instead of testing it.
Only the last row addresses MNAR, and it does so by making you state an assumption out loud rather than by producing evidence.
Acting on the result

What each outcome lets you do next The reason to run any of this is the row it puts you in.

01Nothing predicts missingness

Deletion is defensible and imputation is optional. Say which test you ran and at what sample size, because a small sample fails to reject almost anything.

02Observed columns predict it

Deletion is now biased. Impute or weight, and the predictors that came out significant are exactly the columns the imputation model has to include.

03You suspect the value drives its own absence

No test settles it. Fit under MAR, then repeat across a range of δ and report the span. Say plainly which numbers survive the whole range.

04You cannot tell

The common case. Write down what would have decided it, collect that next time, and carry the ambiguity into the writeup rather than resolving it silently.

Collect the reason for non-response while you collect the data. A single "why did you skip this" field turns an untestable MNAR problem into an observed column, which is the only clean way this argument ever ends.

Recap

What to take from this lecture Five things, in the order they will save you time.

01Tests rule out MCAR, and stop there

All four rejected MCAR for both non-MCAR columns, and all four gave the MNAR column a MAR verdict.

02Failing to reject is not proof

gender passed at p = 0.25 on 28 missing values. A small sample fails to reject almost anything, so quote n beside the p-value.

03The logistic fit is the one that pays

Its significant predictors are the columns your imputation model must include. The other tests give you a verdict; this one gives you a variable list.

04A predictor can inherit the signal

Age looked like the driver of missing health scores because older people score lower. The real driver was the score. Correlation among your columns is how MNAR disguises itself.

05For MNAR, price it

Pattern mixture turns the untestable part into one number, δ. Report the estimate across a defensible range and say whether the conclusion holds throughout.

Before Lecture 3 Four exercises on survey.csv and on your own scraped file.

  1. Reproduce the three verdicts. Run the permutation test yourself at 5,000 shuffles and confirm the p-values on slide 8. Then rerun with 200 shuffles and report how much the answer moves.
  2. Break Little's test. Take survey.csv, keep the first 60 rows, and rerun it. Report the statistic, the p-value, and what changed about your conclusion.
  3. Fit the missingness model on your own data. Pick a column with gaps in a file you scraped. Regress its indicator on every complete column and report the significant predictors, with odds ratios.
  4. Price an MNAR worry. For one column, choose a plausible range of δ, compute the estimate at both ends, and write two sentences on whether your conclusion survives the range.
