MCAR

Concept Complete-case analysis is already unbiased here, so imputation buys sample size rather than correctness.

What the verdict licenses

The rows that lost a value are a random sample, so filling from the observed distribution leaves the mean where it was.

Why fill at all

To keep rows that carry data in their other columns, and to feed PCA, k-means and most of scikit-learn, which refuse incomplete rows.

Unbiased for the mean does not mean harmless. Both methods in this section leave the spread or the correlations wrong, and the scoreboard shows by how much.

The two methods this section covers

MethodWhat it writes into a missing value
Mean, median, mode One number, the same for every missing row. The simplest thing that preserves the centre of the column.
Hot deck A real value copied from another row. Random draw drops the restriction on which row.
Each is legitimate under MCAR, and each turns up on MAR columns where the licence does not extend.
MCAR · Method 1: mean, median and mode

Intuition The income column has 66 missing values, and each one takes the middle of the 334 that were reported.

The move

Compute one summary from the 334 observed values and write it into all 66 gaps. The column's centre survives untouched, because you filled with the centre.

Nothing about the row being filled enters the calculation. A 22-year-old and a 64-year-old receive the same income.
That is the method's defence under MCAR and its whole problem everywhere else.

Which summary for which column

SummaryUse onBecause
MeanNumeric, roughly symmetric Preserves the column total exactly.
MedianNumeric, skewed Income has skew 1.91, so the mean sits above most of the file.
ModeCategorical The only one of the three defined on gender.
MCAR · Method 1: mean, median and mode

Mechanism One statistic computed on the observed rows, broadcast into every missing row.

x̂i = x̄obs for every i with Ri = 1 x̄obs mean of the n−m observed values m number of gaps, here 66 x̂i what lands in row i

Two consequences that follow from the formula

The filled mean equals the observed mean exactly. And the filled variance falls by a factor of about (n−m)/n, because 66 values now sit at distance zero from the centre.

In Python

from sklearn.impute import SimpleImputer

imp = SimpleImputer(strategy='mean')      # or 'median',
df[['income']] = imp.fit_transform(df[['income']])   # 'most_frequent'

# what it does, in one line of pandas
df['income'] = df.income.fillna(df.income.mean())
SimpleImputer fits on the training split and applies the same constant to the test split. Calling fit_transform on the whole file leaks the test values into the training mean.
Both lines above are the entire method. Its cost is on the next three slides, and none of it shows up in the code.

MCAR · Method 2: hot deck

Intuition Rather than invent a number, copy one from a row that resembles the row you are filling.

Random draw: the same idea with no resemblance required
Drawing from the whole column keeps the spread (97,851 against a truth of 99,039) and destroys the structure: corr falls to +0.607 and RMSE reaches 127,361, the worst on the board.

The move

Every filled value is a real observation that occurred in this survey, so it is plausible on its own terms. No formula produces it and no value falls outside the observed range.

Hot deck restricts the draw to a donor pool of similar rows. Random draw is the special case where the pool is the whole column.
The figure is the argument for the restriction. Realistic values in the wrong rows are still wrong.
MCAR · Method 2: hot deck

Mechanism Define a distance on the complete columns, take the nearest donor, copy its value.

d(i, j) = √ ∑k ( zik − zjk )² k the complete columns, here age and education_years z each standardised to sd 1, so neither column dominates by its units x̂i = xj* where j* is the nearest observed row
Standardising matters. Left raw, income-sized numbers would swamp age and every donor would be chosen on one column.

Three choices the method leaves open

  • Pool size. One donor copies noise as well as signal. Five donors averaged is k-NN, on slide 23.
  • With or without replacement. Sampling without replacement stops one donor filling many gaps, and runs out when the pool is small.
  • Class-based or metric. Survey practice often splits into cells (age band × region) and draws inside the cell, which needs no distance at all.

None of the three has a safe default

Each is a modelling decision and each changes which value lands in the gap. Record the choice in the log beside the method name.

Z = (X[['age','education_years']] - mu) / sd
for i in missing:
    j = np.argmin(((Z[observed] - Z[i])**2).sum(1))
    df.loc[i,'income'] = df.income[observed[j]]

MCAR · Method 2: hot deck

Assumptions 4 conditions behind copying a neighbour's value.

AssumptionWhat happens when it fails
Similar on the complete columns means similar on the missing one The whole method rests here. If income depended on something outside age and education_years, the nearest donor is near on the wrong axes.
The donor pool is dense enough A row at the edge of the cloud has no close neighbour, and the nearest donor is simply the least distant of several bad ones. Read the distance itself and treat a large one as a flag.
The distance is meaningful Euclidean on standardised columns treats a year of age and a year of education as equivalent. That is a modelling choice made silently by the default.
Donor reuse is acceptable With replacement, one unusual row can be copied into many gaps, and its value gains weight it never earned.
MCAR · Method 2: hot deck

Drawbacks RMSE 53,866 against regression's 48,295, and one filled dataset either way.

The range is capped by the observed values

Every filled value already exists in the column, so hot deck stays inside the observed range. A row whose true income sits above the maximum observed cannot be reached.

One donor carries one donor's noise

The copied value contains that person's idiosyncrasy as well as the signal, which is why RMSE lands above the model-based methods.

The output is still a single filled file. Analyse it and every standard error you compute treats 66 guesses as 66 measurements. That flaw is shared by every method up to slide 32.
Categorical columns are where hot deck earns its place. Filling gender by copying a neighbour keeps the 53.8/46.2 split that mode imputation destroyed.

What the MAR section changes

Neither method so far used a model of income. The next three fit one, which buys accuracy and introduces a new way to be wrong.

MAR

Concept Missingness depends on age, so conditioning on age removes the bias the last two methods carried.

What the verdict licenses

Lecture 2 found OR 0.948 per year on income.isna() ~ age. The age that predicts the gap also predicts the income, so a model on age can put back what the gap removed.

Complete case gives 137,617 against a truth of 129,782. Every method in this section is trying to close that 7,835.
The bias closes only if age is the whole story. Lecture 2 could not test that, and it returns in the MNAR section.

The three methods this section covers

MethodWhat it writes into a missing value
Regression A model fitted on the complete rows, predicted once per row. No scatter.
Stochastic regression The same prediction with a drawn residual added, so the values scatter like real ones.
k-NN The average of the k nearest complete rows. A model with no equation.
All three run on age and education_years.
MAR · Method 3: regression

Intuition Older and better-educated respondents earn more. Learn that rule from the 334 who answered and apply it to the 66 who did not.

The move

Treat the gapped column as an outcome and the complete columns as predictors. Fit on the rows that have all three, predict on the rest.

Mean imputation is this method with no predictors. Every filled row gets the intercept, which is the mean.
The gain over mean imputation comes from the predictors carrying information. On an unpredictable column the two coincide.

Why the fit is on log income

  • Income has skew 1.91; its logarithm has skew 0.05.
  • A linear model on the raw column would predict negative incomes at the young end.
  • Fit on log(income), predict, exponentiate. The 128,145 quoted in Lecture 2 is this method.
Exponentiating a prediction of the log returns the median, not the mean, of the fitted distribution. On a skewed column that is the more useful of the two.
MAR · Method 3: regression

Mechanism Least squares on the complete rows, then one prediction written into each missing value.

β̂ = (X′obs Xobs)−1 X′obs log(yobs) x̂i = exp( xi′ β̂ ) for every gap i X intercept, age, education_years obs the 334 rows with an income no error term appears anywhere

The missing term is the point

A real income is x′β plus a residual. This method writes down the first part and drops the second, so every filled value is a row's expected income rather than an income.

In Python

obs = df.income.notna()
X   = df[['age','education_years']]

b = LinearRegression().fit(X[obs], np.log(df.income[obs]))
df.loc[~obs,'income'] = np.exp(b.predict(X[~obs]))
IterativeImputer in scikit-learn runs this in a loop across every column with gaps, using the others as predictors. It is regression imputation, applied round-robin until the values settle.
Its default sample_posterior=False makes it deterministic, so it inherits every drawback on slide 19.

MAR · Method 3: regression

Assumptions 4 conditions behind the fitted values, on top of the usual regression diagnostics.

AssumptionWhat happens when it fails
The mechanism is MAR on these predictors The model can only use columns you have. If the reason for the gap sits outside them, the fit is confident and biased, which is slide 28.
The relationship holds outside the observed rows The model is fitted where data exists and applied where it does not. The 66 missing rows are younger than average, so the fit is extrapolating into the thin end of the age range.
The predictors are complete Here they are. When they are not, you need a first imputation to run the second, which is the loop IterativeImputer automates and the place its errors compound.
Only the conditional mean is wanted The method returns E[y | x] by construction. Every use of the filled column that needs its spread is being handed a number that has none.
MAR · Method 3: regression

Drawbacks corr +0.726 against a truth of +0.719: the filled file is more strongly related than the population it came from.

Correlation is inflated, not damaged

Mean imputation pushed corr down to +0.638 by adding structureless points. This method pushes it up past the truth by adding 66 points that lie on a perfect line.

The spread is still short

sd 96,132 against 99,039. Better than mean imputation's 91,762 and still missing the residual variance the model threw away.

Both faults have the same cause. A real income is a prediction plus a residual, and 66 rows were given the prediction alone.
An analyst who inherits this file will report a relationship between age and income that is stronger than the one in the population, with a confidence interval that is too narrow.

The fix is one line

Draw a residual and add it. That is the next method, and it makes every per-value guess worse.

MAR · Method 4: stochastic regression

Intuition Real people scatter around the fitted curve, so filled people should too.

The move

Take the regression prediction, then draw one residual from the same distribution the observed residuals came from and add it. Each gap gets a plausible income rather than an average one.

x̂i = exp( xi′β̂ + εi ), εi ~ N(0, σ̂²) σ̂ residual sd from the fit, on the log scale
Mechanism and assumptions are regression's, taught on slides 16 and 18. The only change is the term that was dropped there.

What adding noise is expected to cost

  • Each individual guess gets worse. You had the best available point prediction and you moved it.
  • The dataset gets right. Spread and correlation return to where the population had them.
  • These pull in opposite directions, and the scoreboard on slide 21 measures both.
Which of the two you want depends on what the file is for. Predicting one person's income is a different job from estimating a population.

MAR · Method 4: stochastic regression

Drawbacks Run it twice and you get two different files, and neither one reports that.

Income before and after stochastic regression
Compare slide 8. The 66 values spread across the range and leave the shape of the distribution intact, which is what the added residual bought.

The result depends on the seed

The filled column is one draw from a distribution of possible columns. Change the seed and every one of the 66 values changes, along with every statistic computed from them.

And the file still does not say so. One dataset, analysed once, produces a standard error that treats the 66 draws as measurements.
That variability is information, not a nuisance. Keeping it is what the last section of this lecture is about.
MAR · Method 5: k-nearest neighbours

Intuition Find the k rows most like this one and average what they reported.

The move

Hot deck takes one donor. This takes k of them and averages, so a single unusual neighbour cannot decide the value on its own.

It fits no equation, so it needs no functional form. A relationship that curves, or turns around mid-range, is handled without being specified.

The dial between Methods 1 and 2

k = 1 is hot deck: keeps the spread, copies one person's noise. k as large as the file is mean imputation: every gap gets the column average.

Averaging k values is also what pulls every filled point toward the middle of the cloud, which is slide 27.
Both scores against k, on the same 66 gaps
RMSE bottoms out at k = 3 (42,839); the spread is closest to the truth at k = 1 (97,776). The two goals want different settings of the same dial.
MAR · Method 5: k-nearest neighbours

Mechanism The same distance as hot deck, then a mean over the k closest rows.

x̂i = (1/k) ∑j ∈ Nk(i) xj Nk(i) the k observed rows nearest to i under d(i, j) from slide 10 weights optional: 1/d gives closer donors more say
Distance-weighted averaging sits between k = 1 and plain k-NN, and moves the spread back a little toward hot deck's.

In Python

from sklearn.impute import KNNImputer

cols = ['age','education_years','income']
Z = StandardScaler().fit_transform(df[cols])      # scale first
out = KNNImputer(n_neighbors=5).fit_transform(Z)
Scale before you call it. KNNImputer uses plain Euclidean distance on the columns as given. Left unscaled, income in rupees dwarfs age in years and every neighbour is chosen on income alone.
It also computes distances on the partly observed rows themselves, using whichever columns two rows share. That is more than the hot deck on slide 10 does.

What the default hides

KNNImputer() runs with n_neighbors=5 and uniform weights on unscaled columns. Two of those three are choices worth making deliberately.

MAR · Method 5: k-nearest neighbours

Assumptions 4 conditions, three of them shared with hot deck and one that is worse here.

AssumptionWhat happens when it fails
The columns are on comparable scales Unscaled, the column with the largest units chooses every neighbour. This is the most common way KNNImputer is used wrongly, and it raises no error.
Neighbours exist in every direction Distance stops discriminating as columns are added. With 2 predictors the nearest 5 rows are genuinely close; with 30 they are not, and the average drifts toward the column mean.
k suits the density One k is applied to the whole file. In a sparse region 5 neighbours reach much further than in a dense one, and nothing in the output records that.
Averaging is meaningful for the column Defined for numbers, not for categories. The average of Male and Female is not a value the column can hold, so categorical columns need a vote instead.
MAR · Method 5: k-nearest neighbours

Drawbacks Lowest RMSE on the board, and the spread and correlation are still wrong.

Winning RMSE is not the goal it looks like

RMSE rewards guessing each value close. Averaging five neighbours does that well and reproduces a distribution badly, because averages vary less than what they average.

Cost grows with the file

Every gap is compared against every complete row, so the work grows with their product. On 400 rows it is instant; on a million it is the reason people reach for a model.

Five methods in, one filled dataset every time. The RMSE column has a winner, the sd and corr columns have a different one, and no row carries an honest standard error.

Concept Every method so far fills from the observed columns, and on an MNAR column the driver is not among them.

What the verdict forbids

MNAR · Method 6: pattern mixture

Mechanism μ = μobs + πδ, with δ supplied rather than fitted.

μ = μobs + πδ π 144/400 = 0.360, counted μobs 87.03, measured δ how far below the observed group the missing group sits δ = −8 → 84.15 δ = −13.09 → 82.31
Lecture 2 built this as a sensitivity tool. Used as an imputation method, the same δ fills every gap at μobs + δ plus scatter.

The only row that reaches the truth

It reaches it because δ = −13.09 was chosen to. No feature of the file points there, and on a real survey there is no marker to follow.

This method buys a visible assumption rather than a better estimate. δ sits on the page instead of inside whichever imputer was installed.
Choose δ from how the question was asked and who declined it. Health scores are withheld by people who score badly, so δ is negative.
MNAR · Method 6: pattern mixture

Drawbacks The estimate is a function of a number the data cannot check.

It does not produce a filled file to hand on

The output is a range of estimates over a range of δ. Tools that want 400 complete rows are no better served.

One δ for the whole column

The formula assumes the missing group sits a fixed distance below the observed one. Where the shift differs by subgroup, the analysis has to be run inside each.

What to report. The estimate at each end of a δ range you can defend, and whether your conclusion changes anywhere inside it. A conclusion that survives the whole range has priced the MNAR worry.
Chasing a sample of the 144 non-respondents is the only move in this lecture that produces evidence rather than an assumption.

One flaw is still unaddressed

Every method so far hands you one file. The last section is what that costs even when the mechanism is MAR.

Multiple imputation · Method 7

Intuition Fill the file several times, analyse each copy, and let the disagreement between them become part of the answer.

The move

Slide 22 showed that stochastic regression gives a different file on every seed. Rather than pick one and forget the others, keep m of them.

Run the analysis you actually wanted on each of the m files. You now have m estimates instead of one.
Their average is the estimate. How far apart they are is how much the missing data cost you, which no single file can report.

The flaw it fixes, in one comparison

single stochastic regression income mean 128,473 SE 4,900 and the SE would be the same if all 400 incomes were measured multiple imputation, m = 20 income mean 129,075 SE 4,996 the extra 96 is the part that comes from not knowing 66 values
On this column the correction is small. Slide 34 shows the column where it is not, and what decides the size.
Multiple imputation · Method 7 · Rubin (1987)

Mechanism m draws, m analyses, and one pooling rule that adds the between-draw variance back.

1 impute m filled files, each drawing β and σ² too, not just ε 2 analyse the same model on each, giving Q̂₁ .. Q̂m and U₁ .. Um 3 pool Q̄ = (1/m) ∑ Q̂k Ū = (1/m) ∑ Uk within B = var(Q̂k) between T = Ū + (1 + 1/m) B total
Step 1 is where it differs from slide 20. Stochastic regression draws ε from a fixed β̂. This draws β and σ² from their posteriors first, so the m files disagree about the model as well as the noise.
The three steps

In Python

from sklearn.experimental import enable_iterative_imputer
from sklearn.impute import IterativeImputer

ests = []
for k in range(20):
    imp = IterativeImputer(sample_posterior=True, random_state=k)
    ests.append(analyse(imp.fit_transform(X)))

Q = np.mean(ests)
T = np.mean(within) + (1 + 1/20)*np.var(ests, ddof=1)
sample_posterior=True is the flag that makes IterativeImputer usable here. Left at its default it returns the same file every time and step 3 has nothing to pool.
R's mice does all three steps, including the pooling, which scikit-learn leaves to you.

Why the analysis goes inside the loop

Step 2 runs the model you actually wanted on each filled file. Pooling the filled values first and analysing once collapses the m files back into one and discards B, which is the term the method exists to compute.

Where the correction is worth having
λ = (1+1/m)B / T is the share of the answer's uncertainty that comes from the gaps. It sets how much pooling changes the standard error.
Multiple imputation · Method 7

Assumptions 4 conditions behind the pooled estimate and its standard error.

AssumptionWhat happens when it fails
The mechanism is MAR Pooling corrects the standard error, never the bias. On health_score it returns 86.03 with an honest interval around a number that is 3.71 points wrong, which is worse than being obviously wrong.
The imputation model is at least as rich as the analysis model A variable left out of the imputation is forced toward zero correlation with the filled column. Include every variable the later analysis will use, including its outcome.
The draws are proper Both β and σ² must be drawn, not fixed at their estimates. Fixing them makes B too small and the pooled standard error too narrow, which is the flaw being fixed.
m is large enough for the λ you have B is itself estimated from m numbers. At λ = 0.25, m = 5 leaves the standard error visibly unstable; m = 20 does not.
Multiple imputation · Method 7

Drawbacks No single filled file to hand on, and on income the correction is 96 rupees.

It changes what a deliverable is

The output is m files and a pooling rule, not a clean CSV. A team that wants one table gets the pooled estimates instead of the data.

The payoff scales with λ, not with effort

On income, λ = 0.045 and twenty imputations move the standard error from 4,883 to 4,996. On health, λ = 0.254 and the same work moves it 16%.

It is still the only method on the board with an honest standard error. The other seven report intervals computed as though 66 guesses were 66 measurements.
Every model choice is made twice, for the imputation and for the analysis, and the two have to agree. That is the real cost.
Use it when missing values are a large share of the column, when an interval matters for a decision, or when the result goes into a paper.
