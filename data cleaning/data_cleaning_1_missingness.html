Orientation · 1 of 2

Cleaning is not tidying The number everyone quotes is the least interesting thing about this topic. The interesting thing is what you are actually doing when you "clean" — which is not housekeeping, and is not reversible.

Where a data scientist's time goes
Two of the six bars are not analysis. Cleaning and collecting together take 79% of the working week — the whole job of getting data into a state where the other four bars are even possible.
Read it honestly. This is a self-report survey of around eighty people, repeated in every slide deck since — a mood, not a measurement. The same report has 57% naming cleaning the least enjoyable part of the job. The claim these numbers support is not "cleaning takes 60% of your time"; it is "nobody plans for this, and everybody spends real time on it."
What cleaning actually is. A dataset is a set of claims about the world. Cleaning changes those claims. When you fill a blank, you are asserting a value that was never measured. When you delete a row, you are asserting it should not count. Both are inferences, and both can be wrong in ways the file will never tell you.

Three things that follow

  • A cleaned file is not more true than the raw one. It is the raw file plus your assumptions. Keep the raw one.
  • Every fix needs a reason you could defend. "It looked wrong" is not a reason. "Values before Oct 2011 were never published" is.
  • The same defect can need opposite treatments. A gap caused by a broken sensor and a gap caused by a refusal to answer are the same blank on screen and different problems entirely.
So the module is organised around causes, not tools. Not "here is imputation, here is outlier detection" — but "here is a symptom; what could have produced it; what does each cause let you do".

Why this happens. read_csv only converts a cell to NaN if it matches its list of null tokens — the empty string, NA, N/A, null, NaN and a few more. A lone hyphen is not on that list, so it stays a one-character string. One string in a column is enough to make the whole column object, which is why OPEN is not a number and CLOSE is.
The dtype was the tell. You did not need to know about the hyphen. A price column that arrives as object is a column with something non-numeric in it, and finding out what is a one-line question: df.OPEN[~df.OPEN.str.match(r"^[\d.]+$")].unique().

And a column that is not data

RequestNumber is the server's pagination handle. It describes your scrape, not the index. It leaks the fact that the file was assembled from 22 separate requests — useful for auditing the collection, meaningless as a feature, and a trap for any model that gets handed the raw frame.

Mechanisms · 1 of 6

The same gap, three defensible answers Here is the situation you are actually in. A column is 35% empty. What do you do? The question has no answer until you say why it is empty — and then it has an obvious one.

The observation

A numeric column. 5,463 rows. 1,932 of them blank. Nothing else known.

There is no method that is correct here, because "correct" is not a property of the method. It is a property of the match between the method and the cause.
This is why the next five slides are about causes and not about code. Rubin's 1976 framework gives the three causes names, and the names are the whole reason the framework is still in use fifty years later.
If the cause wasThen the honest response is
The logger dropped packets at random Drop those rows, or fill them from the rest of the column. Your estimates stay unbiased either way; you have simply lost some precision. This is the easy case, and it is rare.
The feed only carried this field after a certain date Do not fill anything. Restrict the analysis to the period where the field exists, or use a column that spans the whole history. Filling here would be inventing eight years of prices.
Values were suppressed when they were extreme Nothing you compute from the observed rows is safe, including the mean. You need an explicit assumption about the suppressed values, and you must report how the answer changes as that assumption changes.
Same blank cells. Same count. Three different obligations. A colleague who fills all three with the column mean has produced one clean-looking file and two wrong answers, with no error message anywhere.
Mechanisms · 2 of 6 · Rubin (1976)

MCAR, MAR, MNAR Write R for the missingness indicator — one per cell, 1 if present, 0 if missing. Yobs is what you can see, Ymis is what you cannot. The three mechanisms are three answers to one question: what does R depend on?

Missing completely at randomMCAR

P(R | Yobs, Ymis) = P(R)
Missingness depends on nothing in the dataset — not on what you can see, not on what you cannot. The blanks are a random subset of the rows.
Consequence: your remaining data is a smaller but still representative sample. Means, correlations and models stay unbiased. You lose precision, not correctness.
Looks likeA logger drops packets when a buffer overflows · a tray of samples is knocked over · a page of paper forms is lost in transit

Missing at randomMAR

P(R | Yobs, Ymis) = P(R | Yobs)
Missingness depends on things you observed, but not on the missing value itself. Once you condition on the observed variables, what is left is random.
Consequence: naive estimates are biased, but the bias is recoverable — because everything driving it is in the file. This is what regression imputation and MICE assume.
Looks likeYounger respondents skip the income question · a field only exists after a format change · one clinic records fewer follow-ups than another

Missing not at randomMNAR

P(R | Yobs, Ymis) depends on Ymis
Missingness depends on the value that is missing. The reason a cell is blank is bound up with what would have been in it.
Consequence: no amount of conditioning on observed data fixes it, because the driver was never recorded. You need an assumption from outside the data, and you must report how the answer moves when it changes.
Looks likeHigh earners decline to state salary · the sickest patients drop out of the trial · a sensor saturates and records nothing above its range
The word "random" is doing damage in two of these names. MAR is not random — it is systematic, and systematically explainable. Read the names as: MCAR = depends on nothing; MAR = depends on what you have; MNAR = depends on what you do not have.
Exit polls · 1 of 3

One survey, three ways to lose an answer You are outside a polling booth on counting day, asking every voter who they voted for, plus age, gender and income. Some people do not answer. The blanks look identical on your clipboard. Which mechanism produced them decides whether your poll is usable.

The setup

Booth exit. Voluntary. You record vote, age, gender, income. Refusals and walk-offs leave gaps.

The only question that matters: is the reason someone did not answer connected to the answer they would have given?

Why exit polls

Because the truth arrives a few hours later, when the votes are counted. Almost no survey lets you check your own bias against reality. This one does — which is why its failures are so well documented.

MCARMissing completely at random
What happensWhy it is MCAR
Voters leave in a hurry
late for work, bus arriving		 Being in a rush has nothing to do with who they voted for, how old they are, or what they earn. It is the clock, and the clock does not know their politics.
Forms blow away
5% of the pile lost to wind		 The wind does not select. A lost form is as likely to have said Party A as Party B.

What it costs you

Sample size, and nothing else. With 5% lost at random you have 95% of a good survey. Your vote-share estimate is still centred on the truth; the confidence interval is slightly wider.

Why you rarely get it

Real non-response is almost never this clean. People who are in a hurry are not a random slice of the electorate either — commuters skew younger and urban. Genuine MCAR is a machine failing, not a person choosing.

Exit polls · 2 of 3

MAR — the refusal is systematic, and you can see why Now suppose younger voters refuse the vote-choice question far more often than older ones. That is not random. But every bit of it is explained by a column you already have.

MAR30% of voters under 25 skip the vote question. 5% of voters over 25 do. But within any single age group, who refuses has nothing to do with how they voted.
Age groupRefusalWhat that does to a naive tally
Under 2530% Young voters are now under-represented. If they lean differently from older voters — and they usually do — your headline number shifts towards the older group.
25 and over5% Barely touched.
And it is fixable. You know each respondent's age, including the ones who refused the vote question — they answered that part. So you know exactly how much each group is under-counted, and you can reweight, or model the vote as a function of age and impute. The information needed to undo the damage is in the file.

Formally

P(refuse | vote, age) = P(refuse | age). Once age is in the model, the missingness carries no further information. That equality is the entire definition, and it is also exactly what multiple imputation and maximum likelihood need in order to be valid.

The catch that makes L2 necessary. MAR is a statement about a variable you did not observe — the vote you never got. You cannot verify the equality above from your data. You can make it more plausible by measuring more covariates, and that is the practical advice: collect the things you suspect drive non-response, because they are what buy you MAR later.
Which methods rely on this. Regression imputation, kNN imputation, MICE, and full-information maximum likelihood are all MAR methods. Lecture 3 works through what each of them assumes and what each of them breaks.
Exit polls · 3 of 3

MNAR — the refusal is about the answer Last version. Voters for one particular party are embarrassed to say so, and refuse at ten times the rate of everyone else. Nothing else about them is unusual.

MNAR50% of Party X voters refuse the vote question. 5% of everyone else does. Age, gender and income distributions are identical across the two groups.
You tryWhat happens
Reweight by age, gender, income Nothing. Those distributions already match — the refusers are demographically ordinary. Your weights are all 1 and the bias survives untouched.
Impute vote from covariates Worse than nothing. The model learns the vote pattern of people who answered, and confidently fills the refusers with it — which is precisely the pattern they do not follow.
Collect more covariates Only helps if one of them correlates with the hidden vote. If the embarrassment is about the vote itself, no demographic rescues you.

Formally

P(refuse | vote, age, ...) still depends on vote after conditioning on everything you observed. The driver of missingness is the missing value. It is not in the file, and it cannot be got out of the file.

What you can actually do. Not solve it — bound it. Assume Party X refusers voted Party X with probability p, redo the analysis across a plausible range of p, and report the range. That is a sensitivity analysis, and for MNAR it is usually the whole deliverable. Or go and get external data: a re-contact study, an official count, a validated sub-sample.
The honest summary of the three. MCAR costs you precision. MAR costs you an assumption you can support. MNAR costs you the ability to give a single number at all.
Exit polls · what the wrong assumption cost

2016: a MAR correction applied to MNAR missingness US presidential election polling and exit polling. The methods were standard, the weighting was competent, and the result was wrong in a direction that was entirely predictable from the mechanism.

  1. Response rates were lower among rural, non-college-educated white voters — a group that broke heavily for Trump.
  2. Pollsters reweighted by age, gender, race and region. That is the correct procedure — if the missingness is MAR given those variables.
  3. Education was under-weighted or omitted in many state-level polls, and it was the variable most strongly tied to both response and vote.
  4. The residual missingness was MNAR. Willingness to answer was tied to the vote itself, not only to demography.
  5. The correction could not reach it. Reweighting redistributes people you have. It cannot conjure people whose absence is caused by the answer they would have given.
The result. Samples over-represented college-educated voters, who leaned Clinton, and under-represented non-college white voters, who leaned Trump. Every published margin tilted the same way — not by accident, but because a single shared assumption was wrong in a single shared direction.

The lesson that generalises

A missing-data assumption is not a technicality buried in a methods appendix. It is a load-bearing claim about the world, and when it is wrong, every downstream number is wrong in the same direction at once. Correlated error does not average out across polls, models or teams — which is why the whole industry missed it together.

What changed afterwards. Education entered the weighting scheme as standard, and sensitivity analysis over non-response assumptions became a normal part of reporting. Both are MNAR responses: not a fix, but an honest accounting of how far the answer could move.

Mechanisms · 4 of 6 · the central difficulty

MAR and MNAR look the same until you condition Both columns showed a trend against age. Both also show a trend against their own hidden values — MAR included. The definition is not "no relationship with the missing value"; it is no relationship left once you condition on what you observed. Here is that difference, measured.

% missing, split by the row's true value, within each age band
income · MAR 18-29 30-41 42-53 54-65 above median 28.8 20.8 11.3 4.3 below median 35.8 12.2 11.3 4.3 ~~~~ no consistent gap ~~~~ health · MNAR 18-29 30-41 42-53 54-65 above median 0.0 6.8 8.3 25.0 below median 42.9 60.4 55.2 79.2 ^^^ a gap in every band ^^^
Income is MAR. Inside an age band, whether someone earns above or below the median makes no difference to whether they answered — 28.8 against 35.8, then 11.3 against 11.3. Age explained all of it, and age is in the file.
Health is MNAR. Inside every age band the low scorers are withheld five to ten times as often. Age explained none of it. Add every other observed column and the gap stays, because the driver is the score itself.
Neither table on the left can be computed on real data. Both split rows by a value that is missing for exactly the rows being counted. They exist here only because the data was simulated — which is the entire reason MNAR is untestable.

The verdict

Missingness is a deterministic function of an observed variable, the date. Fit a logistic regression of OPEN.isna() on the date and it separates the classes perfectly — training accuracy 1.0. Missingness depends on observed data only, so by definition this is MAR.

And the cause is knowable, which is better than a classification. The exchange only began publishing open, high and low for its indices in October 2011. Before that it published the closing level alone. The gap is not a failure of the scrape or of the exchange — those numbers were never released, so there is nothing that went missing.
Notice the order of work. The statistics said MAR. The documentation said why, and the why is what tells you what to do next. Reach for domain knowledge first; it is faster than a test and it answers a better question.
Mechanisms · 6 of 6 · the sting

MAR does not mean fillable The file is MAR. Every textbook says MAR is the case where imputation works. Here it does not work at all — and understanding why is worth more than any imputation method in Lecture 3.

There is no overlap. To learn a rule for OPEN from date, you need dates where OPEN is sometimes present and sometimes missing. Here, every date is one or the other. The predictor that explains the missingness perfectly is the predictor that leaves nothing to learn from.
If you did it anywayWhat you would get
df.OPEN.fillna(df.OPEN.mean()) Eight years of 2004–2011 opens set to a 2011–2025 average, on an index that went from a base of 1,000 to 11,168 over the period. Every one of them wrong by a factor.
Regress OPEN on CLOSE, fit on 2011+ An R² near 1 and a comfortable feeling. You have extrapolated a modern intraday relationship eight years into a period with different circuit rules, different constituents and a different tick size. The fit statistic cannot see any of that.
Forward-fill from the previous row There is no previous row. The gap runs to the start of the file.

What to do instead

Restrict the window. If the analysis needs open/high/low, it starts on 2011-10-03 and you say so in the writeup. Or change the measure. CLOSE is complete across all 5,463 rows — if daily returns will do, you keep the full 2004–2025 history and impute nothing.

The general rule. The mechanism tells you whether filling is defensible. Whether it is possible is a separate question about overlap — do rows with the value and rows without it share a region of the predictor space? Structural gaps, where a field simply did not exist yet, are MAR and un-imputable at the same time, and they are extremely common in scraped and historical data.
Carry this into Lecture 3. Every method there will happily return numbers for this column. None of them will warn you. The check has to be yours.
Seeing it · 1 of 3

Counts tell you how much. Pictures tell you what shape. isna().sum() gives one number per column and throws away the only thing that identifies a mechanism: which rows, and which columns together. Three views recover it, and each answers a different question.

01Matrix

Question: where in the file are the gaps?

One column per variable, rows top to bottom in their natural order. A solid block means a period or a batch. Even speckle means something closer to random. Stripes mean a repeating process — a source, a shift, a sensor.

msno.matrix(df)

02Correlation heatmap

Question: which columns go missing together?

Build a 0/1 indicator per column, correlate the indicators. Near 1 means one defect wearing several column names. Near 0 means independent causes that need separate treatment.

msno.heatmap(df)

03Dendrogram

Question: how many distinct causes are there?

Hierarchical clustering on the same indicators. Columns joining at height zero are one mechanism. Useful when there are forty columns and reading a forty-by-forty heatmap is not.

msno.dendrogram(df)
Order matters, and it is your responsibility. These plots draw rows in the order they sit in the DataFrame. A time series sorted by date shows its structure immediately; the same file shuffled shows none. Before plotting, sort by whatever you think the mechanism might depend on — date, source, batch, respondent id — and plot it again under a different sort if the first tells you nothing.

Speckle, at three different densities. No edges, no alignment between columns — three separate processes, each acting on individual respondents. The density differences are real (36%, 16.5%, 7%) but the picture cannot tell you why any of them happen. For that you need the breakdowns from slide 15.
What the matrix is good at, and what it is not. It finds structure: blocks, edges, stripes, and rows that are blank across the board. It says nothing about mechanism — MAR and MNAR both produce speckle. Use it to rule out MCAR quickly and to spot the structural gaps that no imputation method should ever be pointed at.

What you may do before Lecture 3

Deletion is a method, and it has assumptions too Imputation is next lecture. Deletion is available now, it is the default in almost every modelling library, and most people use it without noticing they have chosen it.

ApproachWhat it doesValid when
Listwise deletion
df.dropna()		 Drop any row with a gap anywhere. Every analysis runs on the same rows, so results are mutually consistent. On the survey: 400 rows becomes 200. Half the sample, for gaps in three columns.MCAR
Pairwise deletion
df.corr()		 Use every row that has the columns each statistic needs. Keeps more data, but each number rests on a different subsample — a correlation matrix built this way can fail to be positive definite and break the model that consumes it.MCAR

The default you did not choose. Most scikit-learn estimators raise on NaN. A groupby, a mean() or a plotting call instead drops rows quietly and returns a number — pairwise deletion is pandas' default, and it carries an MCAR assumption.
df = pd.read_csv("BLUE METAL.csv", na_values=["-"])
len(df), len(df.dropna()), len(df.dropna(subset=["OPEN"]))
(5463, 0, 3531)
An empty DataFrame, with no error. One column is 100% null, so every row has a gap somewhere and every row goes. Drop Index Name first and the same call keeps 3,531. Listwise deletion is only as good as your column list, and it fails silently when that list is wrong.
Report two numbers whenever you delete: how many rows went, and whether the survivors resemble the departed. On survey.csv, dropna() takes 400 rows to 200 and moves mean age 40.9 → 40.0. That comparison is the germ of the formal tests in Lecture 2.
Deletion is sometimes right. Two per cent missing, no structure in the matrix, no difference between kept and dropped — delete, note it, move on.

Recap

What to carry out of this lecture Six things, in the order they will save you time.

01Count what is missing, not what pandas says is missing

On a real file the two differed by 2.1×. Check the dtypes, look for sentinels, and re-read with na_values before reporting any number.

02Look for rows that were never written

Four missing years left no trace in any column. Compare what came back against what you asked for — nothing else will.

03The mechanism decides the method

MCAR depends on nothing, MAR on what you observed, MNAR on what you did not. The three get three different treatments, and choosing wrongly biases every downstream number in the same direction at once.

04MAR and MNAR look alike in your data

Both show trends against observed variables. The difference only shows up against the hidden values — the one comparison you can never run. That is why MNAR is argued for, never demonstrated.

05MAR does not mean fillable

Perfectly explained missingness with no overlap is un-imputable. Ask whether the model has anything to learn from before you reach for a method that will answer regardless.

06Documentation beats inference

A logistic regression classified this file as MAR. One line in the exchange's release notes explained it. Look the cause up first — it is faster, and it answers the question the test cannot.
